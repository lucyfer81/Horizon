## [Claude AI discovers a novel CRISPR-like enzyme system in bacterial DNA.](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-discovers-novel-enzyme-system) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Anthropic&\#x27;s Claude AI autonomously identified a previously undescribed CRISPR-like repeat array adjacent to a reverse transcriptase in a bacterial genome, suggesting the existence of a novel enzyme system. This discovery, named ART, was made in approximately 21 hours using 950 parallel analysis sessions. This demonstrates AI&\#x27;s growing capability to accelerate fundamental scientific discovery by autonomously analyzing complex genomic data, potentially uncovering new biological mechanisms. It highlights a shift towards AI-driven research that could lead to novel tools for biotechnology, similar to how CRISPR revolutionized gene editing. The system was found in jumbo phage genomes and includes an accessory protein of unknown function alongside non-coding repeat arrays. The discovery is framed as an early result from Anthropic&\#x27;s research programs, though some experts note it revolves around a known retron-like reverse transcriptase, suggesting a need for sober interpretation of the novelty.

**Background**: CRISPR \(Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats\) is a bacterial immune system that has been adapted into a powerful gene-editing tool. Bacterial genome annotation is the process of identifying and describing genes and other functional elements within a bacterial DNA sequence, often using tools like Bakta for rapid analysis. Reverse transcriptases are enzymes that can generate DNA from an RNA template, and some are associated with bacterial defense systems like retrons.

## [Analysis Predicts LLM Inference Costs Will Soon Be Cheaper Than a &\#x27;grep&\#x27; Command](https://jyn.dev/tokens-too-cheap-to-meter/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A recent analysis argues that the cost of Large Language Model \(LLM\) inference is falling so rapidly that it will soon be cheaper than the computational cost of a simple &\#x27;grep&\#x27; command, making AI-generated tokens effectively &\#x27;too cheap to meter&\#x27;. The post specifically observes that a call to a model like GPT-5.6 Luna is currently only 4-5 orders of magnitude more expensive than &\#x27;grep&\#x27; and projects this gap will close based on current progress rates. If this prediction holds, it would fundamentally change the economics of AI, unlocking a vast array of new applications that were previously not commercially viable due to cost. It suggests a future where AI capabilities could be embedded ubiquitously and used for tasks currently considered too trivial for LLMs, potentially reshaping software development and human-computer interaction. The analysis acknowledges that the rate of cost decline varies dramatically, with research showing annual reductions ranging from 9x to 900x depending on the performance benchmark. A key counterpoint from the community discussion is Stein&\#x27;s Law, which cautions that such exponential efficiency improvements cannot continue forever, and questions remain about the long-term business model viability for AI infrastructure investors.

**Background**: LLM inference refers to the computational process of generating text output \(tokens\) from a trained model in response to an input prompt. The cost of this process has been falling rapidly due to hardware improvements, algorithmic optimizations, and economies of scale. &\#x27;grep&\#x27; is a classic Unix command-line tool for searching plain-text data sets for lines matching a regular expression, and its computational cost is extremely low, often serving as a baseline for simple text processing operations. The phrase &\#x27;too cheap to meter&\#x27; originated in the 1950s in reference to the anticipated future of nuclear power, suggesting a utility so inexpensive that tracking individual usage would be pointless.

## [Seattle becomes first U.S. city to ban surveillance pricing for groceries.](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/seattle-city-council-votes-to-ban-surveillance-pricing-in-sale-of-groceries/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The Seattle City Council passed CB 121267, the Fair Pricing and Transparency Act, which prohibits retailers from using surveillance data and personal characteristics to set personalized prices for grocery items. This marks the first city-level ban of its kind in the United States. This legislation directly challenges the growing practice of algorithmic surveillance pricing, aiming to protect consumer privacy and ensure fair access to essential goods. It sets a precedent that could inspire similar regulations in other cities and states, potentially reshaping how retailers use personal data for pricing nationwide. The bill specifically targets the use of personal data, such as purchase history or demographics, to charge individuals different prices for the same grocery item. It also permits various discounting practices but requires increased transparency around discounts and places limits on consumer profiling for pricing purposes.

hackernews · ortusdux · Sep 23, 14:04 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49816374)

**Background**: Personalized or surveillance pricing is a strategy where businesses set prices for individuals based on their personal data, such as browsing history, location, or purchase patterns, rather than offering a fixed price to all. This practice, often powered by algorithms, has raised concerns about privacy, discrimination, and fairness, especially for essential goods like groceries. Regulatory bodies like the FTC have warned that such practices may violate consumer protection laws, leading to a growing number of state-level legislative efforts to restrict algorithmic and data-driven pricing.
