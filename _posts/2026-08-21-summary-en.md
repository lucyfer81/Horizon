## [US Citizen Faces Felony Charges for Deleting Phone Data at Border](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/08/21/us/politics/samuel-tunick-deleted-phone-felony.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A US citizen, Samuel Tunick, is facing felony charges for allegedly deleting data from his phone after being ordered to unlock it by Customs and Border Protection \(CBP\) agents. The charges are reportedly based on the federal obstruction of justice statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1519. This case sets a significant legal precedent by potentially criminalizing the act of protecting digital privacy at the border, challenging the limits of the government&\#x27;s search authority. It has major implications for travelers&\#x27; rights, digital security practices, and the balance between national security and personal privacy in the digital age. The charges invoke 18 U.S.C. § 1519, which prohibits destroying records to impede a federal investigation. This legal strategy expands the application of obstruction laws from traditional document destruction to real-time, personal digital data management at ports of entry.

**Background**: Under the &\#x27;border search exception&\#x27; to the Fourth Amendment, U.S. Customs and Border Protection \(CBP\) agents have broad authority to conduct warrantless searches of electronic devices at ports of entry without needing probable cause or reasonable suspicion. CBP policy states that border searches of devices are critical for enforcing customs, immigration, and other laws. Travelers may be asked to provide device passwords, and refusal can lead to device seizure or detention.

## [Researcher discovers critical ENUM/e164.arpa DNS misconfiguration logging sensitive military calls](https://lina.sh/blog/hijacking-e164-arpa) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A security researcher discovered a critical misconfiguration in the global ENUM/e164.arpa DNS system, where a nameserver operated by a company was inadvertently logging queries for sensitive phone numbers, including hundreds of thousands of calls to military bases and other high-security numbers. The researcher documented how this system, intended to map phone numbers to internet addresses, was leaking detailed call metadata due to a default logging configuration. This misconfiguration represents a severe privacy and national security vulnerability, as it exposed sensitive call patterns and potentially operational details of military and government entities to unauthorized logging. It highlights systemic risks in critical but often overlooked internet infrastructure, where default settings and lack of oversight can lead to massive data leaks. The vulnerability stemmed from a nameserver for the e164.arpa domain using default configurations that logged all DNS queries, which included the full E.164 phone numbers being looked up. The researcher&\#x27;s analysis showed the logs contained call data spanning years, revealing the surprising persistence of this issue before discovery.

hackernews · gavide · Aug 21, 13:11 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49387570)

**Background**: ENUM \(E.164 Number Mapping\) is a system that bridges the traditional telephone network \(PSTN\) with the Internet by storing SIP \(Session Initiation Protocol\) addresses or other URIs in the DNS under a special domain, e164.arpa. It allows devices to look up how to route a voice-over-IP \(VoIP\) call to a standard phone number. The e164.arpa domain is a globally delegated DNS namespace specifically for this purpose, where phone numbers are reversed and formatted as domain names for DNS queries.

This matters because it highlights a growing user experience and content quality crisis as AI-generated text proliferates across the web, code reviews, and educational materials. ## [AI Companies Destroy Rare Books After Scanning for Training Data](https://annas-archive.gl/blog/physical-destruction.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Recent investigations reveal that AI companies like Amazon and Anthropic are bulk-buying rare and out-of-print books, using high-speed scanning machines that cut the spines to digitize them, and then discarding the physical copies. This practice, termed &\#x27;destructive digitization,&\#x27; is driven by the need for vast text datasets to train large language models. This raises significant ethical and cultural concerns, as it prioritizes cost-effective data acquisition over the preservation of unique cultural artifacts. The permanent loss of physical books, especially rare editions, could irreversibly diminish the historical and cultural record for future generations. The destructive method, which involves cutting the book spine for efficient scanning, is reportedly up to 10 times cheaper than non-destructive scanning techniques. Companies are reportedly not distinguishing between common and rare books in this process, treating all as a commodity for data extraction.

**Background**: To train advanced AI models like large language models \(LLMs\), companies require massive amounts of high-quality text data. While much data is sourced from the web, books provide dense, long-form, and curated content that is highly valuable for training. Previous large-scale digitization projects, like Google Books, faced legal challenges but typically employed non-destructive methods to preserve the original books.
